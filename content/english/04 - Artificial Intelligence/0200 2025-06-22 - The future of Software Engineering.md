# The future of Software Engineering

![](attachments/ChatGPT%20Image%20Oct%2025,%202025,%2002_49_54%20PM.png)

A couple of days ago, I met my high school Computer Science teacher while waiting in line at the supermarket. She and I spent a lot of time preparing my studies back in the day, and she also happens to be one of my favorite teachers, so we've kept in touch over the years. I often get invited for fireside-chat-style conversations with her and her current students at high school, but this time I was in a rush, so I had to decline the invitation. However, she did have one burning question that we briefly discussed while waiting in line.

What is the future of software engineering and will Computer Science high school students have a job when they graduate, 4-8 years from now, or should they study something else?

Now, to be honest, up to that point, I did not think or care much about that question. I always thought that it will take a while for this issue to catch up with me, and, by the time it does, I'd be starting a bakery, or a farm, or some other stereotypical non-technical activity that old and burnt-out developers do when they move out of Silicon Valley.

However, when the question comes from a high school teenager who aspires to write code for a living, it hits a bit different. I spent a couple of weeks thinking about this and, while no one has a definitive answer anyway, I want to share a couple of thoughts about the future of the Software Engineering career.

First of all: **what do we do**? In my opinion, our role as Software Engineers is to take real world business needs and implement computer solutions that address those needs. How we do that depends on our role, our expertise, our seniority and the needs themselves. Some have to design software architectures, others have to change config files, others have to tweak existing templates, others have to manage expectations, others have to manage humans and others need to write code.

For some reason, of all things, a lot of people appear to agree that writing code is either the unpleasant part, or the hard part, or the *only* part of our job and, for a very long time, they tried to simplify this process, or to replace it altogether.

The most recent incarnation of this attempt is "vibe coding", where the developer does not write code anymore, but instead they give an LLM agent access to the codebase and then they prompt the LLM to write the code. Prompting means giving an LLM instructions in natural language (English) and instructing it what to do. In this new paradigm, the developer does not have to do they "dirty" coding, they just need to "vibe check" that the LLM follows the general direction and that the final result is satisfactory.

This new paradigm blew people's minds and the biggest companies behind code editors that allow you to code like that: Cursor and Windsurf have been valued at $9.9 billion (valuation) and $3 billion (acquisition) respectively, after only a couple of years of operation. Yup, that's billion with a B.

Okay, so people with money are excited, but what does this mean for us, developers?

One conclusion would be that people strongly believe in a future where developers do not write computer instructions in C++, Java, Python and JSON anymore. Instead, they write computer instructions in English, or even their native language. This aspect gets people very excited, very quickly, and gets them thinking that Software Engineering as a career is about to go extinct because anyone could make the computer do whatever they want, using only English.

That could be true. But is it true, tough? Here is an example. These days, anyone could represent themselves in the court of law, using English only, but most people choose not to, either because they do not want to get it wrong, or because they have better things to do with their energy and prefer to trust the lawyers. Similarly, anyone could change a tyre with a wrench and a car jack, but most people still prefer to take the car to a shop because it is faster and safer.

Of course, like any tool, vibe coding will enable many people to achieve things that were not possible before. Like a wrench enabling an ambitious person to change a tire, an LLM will enable a motivated person to create their own website and to change all buttons from red to green. However, I argue that these types of people are a minority of the population and someone driven enough to prompt an LLM to create a website is just as driven as someone who learned HTML and CSS on their own a couple of years ago. Because these people, who no longer need a developer, are a minority, I don't think they will meaningfully change the market.

There is also a different argument to be made. Maybe vibe coding will not fully replace developers, but it will make us, let's say, net 10x more efficient (even though I heard claims of 1,000x or even 1,000,000x). A 10x productivity boost would mean that for every 10 existing developers, 9 of them will become redundant. This would lead to 90% workforce layoffs, which would lead to a surge in supply of tech talent, effectively filling in all available open positions, leading to developers, particularly junior ones not needed anymore.

Recent layoffs from big tech companies: Google, Meta, Microsoft further fuel this fear, and claims from Google's CEO Sundar Pichai that 25% of Google's code is now written by AI appear to confirm the correlation.

While this is a more possible scenario, I am skeptical for a couple of reasons. Based on my own experience and conversations that I had with friends around Silicon Valley, while AI is game changing at automating repetitive coding, such as boilerplate or switch statements, it quickly falls apart when having to make changes in larger codebases, making it useless outside toy examples. There are a couple of technical reasons why this is happening, but the point is that I have yet to see the 10x average increase in productivity. I would be shocked if I even see a 10% increase.

The reason is that writing code was never the bottleneck. Reading code was always harder than writing it and all LLMs are doing is produce more verbose code that takes longer to read and understand, leading to a net 0 increase in productivity.

One last point that I want to make is a twist on Parkinson's Law: "Work expands to fill the available time". That is "Work expands to fully utilize all available developers". Even if we are all 10x more efficient, there is always 10x more work to do.

To conclude what turned out to be a pretty long article, I believe that teenagers who enter the Software Engineering workforce in 4-8 years time should not yet worry about whether or not this career exists by then.

However, a paradigm shift already started and, because of that, Software Engineering will look very different in 4-8 years than it looks today. A totally different set of skills will be needed. Just like writing assembly code was a skill that became redundant in the 50s when Fortran, Lisp and Cobol were introduced, modern programming languages may also become redundant in a few decades. As a developer, you should always learn and always perfect your craft. In fact, as any profession, you should always strive to get better at your craft.
