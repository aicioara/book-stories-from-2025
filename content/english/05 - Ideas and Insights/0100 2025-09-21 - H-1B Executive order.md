# The H-1B Executive Order

Friday, September 19, 2025, the White House released an Executive Order that may or may not affect all H-1B visa holders, which includes me and my wife. The story is still developing, but I want to write my first thoughts, while they are still raw, as this is, for me personally, the most impactful change in legislation since I moved to the United States.

I want to also begin by quickly explaining how immigration and visas works, because it is a complex issue no one really needed to understand, but I hope I can turn it into an enjoyable primer.

## How USA visas work

The United States of America is in the privileged position of being, the most desired country in the world. Many talented people live and work here. Because talent attracts talent, many people want to move here from other places. USA recognized this a very long time ago and created avenues for people to move here if they wanted to. However, USA, like any entity, wants the best outcome for itself and its citizens, so it filters out prospective immigrants, so that it only imports the talent it needs and rejects people who are not a good fit or are deemed to not contribute positively to the society. This filter is called a Visa.

Life is complex and people are complicated, so there is not only one blank visa for everyone. Instead, there are multiple visa categories. Each of them has a different criteria and each of them gives the owner a different set of rights and responsibilities. The fact that there are dozens of different categories makes the entire immigration process very complicated because most people, not even I, are aware of every single one of these visas.

**If you only remember one thing out of this article, let that be: there are multiple visa categories and they are incredibly different from one another. H-1B is one such category.**

In the rest of this section, I will give a few examples, to make this a lot easier and give you a feeling of the options that exist.

## Visa Examples

(I think I am required to say this somewhere, so I'll say it here. I am not a lawyer and this is not immigration advice. While I do my best to make this accurate - as of 2025 - the following content is made for entertainment purposes only. Do not sue me.)

Also, there are dozens of visas and they are mostly boring. The options below is just to give you an idea of the most popular types of visas in tech.

##### B-1/B-2 - Tourist / Business

This is the most common type of visa. It is used by regular citizens who want to visit the USA as a tourist and by workers who need to visit USA on business trips, such as presenting in a conference or meeting their USA coworkers.

Unless you have a shady past, pretty much everyone can get this visa. If you are in a country that has access to the ESTA visa waiver, this visa is not needed anymore (again, for tourist and business reasons).

The key obligation here is that you are not allowed to work at all while in the USA. It means that you are not allowed to be employed as a tourist, but also not allowed to do meaningful work while on a business trip. It also means that you are not allowed to work as a "digital nomad". Just no work at all.

Another obligation is that you should exit the country after 6 months and have to have a valid reason for every re-entry, so you cannot go to Mexico and back for a day every 6 months.

##### J-1 - Foreign Student

J-1 is a visa that is even easier to get than B-1/B-2. It gives you the right to work in the USA for a limited time, up to 1 year. The only requirement is that you must be an active student in your home country.

This visa is very popular with summer interns (generally in tech) and with work-and-travel programs that employ seasonal workers (generally in hospitality).

The only catch is that you must return to your home country at the end of the internship and the internship can be maximum 12 months.

##### F-1 - Domestic Student

While J-1 is for students who study abroad and work in the USA for the summer, F-1 is for students who study in the USA. The visa is relatively easy to get, as long as you've been accepted to a high school, university, PhD, etc, in the USA.

The F-1 also gives the option of an F-1 OPT for 3 years, at the end of the studies, during which time you can work on the F-1 OPT. The only catch is that you need to convert to a work visa before the end of the F-1 OPT, otherwise you have to leave the country.

##### H-1B - Skilled Worker

Finally, getting into the work visas. H-1B is the first one so far that also gives the holder the right to work. H-1B is the most popular such visa, the hardest to get, and the one I have. H-1B is only valid for 6 years, 3 years at the time, but it is dual-intent, meaning that the owner can also apply for a Permanent Residence, which, if accepted, gives you the right to remain and work indefinitely.

For an H-1B, you must have a university degree and you must have a job lined up (meaning you passed the intereview and have signed the employment offer). You can only apply for it in April and you can only start using it in October. That means, an employer must really want you in order to find you in another country, give you an offer and keep the position blocked for up to 1.5 years just for you to eventually join (1.5 years in the worst case means they found you in May, the applied for H-1B in April the following year and got you in October, 0.5 years in best case where they found you in March, applied in April and got you in October).

The catch with the H-1B is that there are only 80,000 such visas every year. To make the process "fair", they introduced a lottery system. With that, even with a university degree, even with an employer willing to wait for you, you still only have a marginal chance to receive the visa. In 2024, there were 780,000 applications, so each applicant had an approximately 10% chance of getting the visa.

The benefit of this visa is that once you get it, you can change employers easily, which makes many employers reluctant to go this route because it also means that all their effort was for nothing if you decide to leave.

In 2019, 75% of applicants of this visa were India nationals, 12% were China nationals and 13% come from the rest of the world, each country representing 1% or less of the applications ([source](https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/data/h-1b-petitions-by-gender-country-of-birth-fy2019.pdf)). Of course, the distribution of a randomly sampled output matches the distribution of the input, which raises questions of the actual fairness of the process.

##### L-1 - Company Transfer

L-1 is another popular type of visa. It allows employers to bring their own employees from a different country to the USA, provided they've been with the company for at least one year. For example, if you worked for Google Bucharest for 1 year, you are eligible to move to California on an L-1 visa. L-1 visas are easy to get and do not have a lottery or a cap.

The catch here is that you are stuck with the company. If you were to leave the company, or be fired, or be laid off, you would have 60 days to pack all of your belongings and leave the United States.

Indirectly, this visa forces you to never leave an employer, as without them, both you and your family would need to move out. Because of the added stress, this is not a desirable visa.

##### O-1 - Special Talent

O-1 is the visa for people with "extraordinary ability" and it is often referred to as the "Einstein Visa".

Quick aside here. Yes, even in 1933, Albert Einstein, as a Swiss citizen, needed a tourist visa to come in the USA. However, Einstein obtained a Permanent Residency directly after the Tourist visa. Given his status at the time, he would have gotten the Permanent Resident status by the president, no questions asked, but he preferred to go through the process most people went through, so he upgraded his Tourist visa to a Permanent Residence just like everyone else. So no, the O-1 visa has nothing to do with Einstein, but it is a cute name.

You can get the visa by matching as many criteria out of a checkbox list, such as winning competitions, publishing papers, having articles written about you, judging competitions, commanding a high salary, membership in exclusive associations. Since many of these criteria sit in somewhat a gray area, this is also a very subjective visa and it is all about the decision of the person that processes your application.

With the H-1B having the current lottery issue, the O-1 is the current favorite visa of tech workers, but lawyers generally try to avoid it because of the additional paperwork required to prove that the applicant is "outstanding". One of the issues with this visa is that it promotes this visa-driven-development, where people end up doing things just because it will tick one more box on the visa scoreboard.

##### K-1 - Fiance

K-1 is the marriage based visa. The idea behind it is that love should not be separated by borders. American citizens have the right to marry whoever they want and be able to live close to them in the USA.

Someone can get this visa by marrying an American Citizen (attention, not a permanent resident or visa holder). While not as popular in tech as in other fields, it is not unheard of for my colleagues at work to have gotten here by marrying a citizen. Generally, someone comes here on an F-1 or J-1 visa as a student, falls in love and marries to have a better visa after they graduate.

However, it is not unheard of for American citizens to travel abroad to find their love and attract them with the promise of this visa. The couple needs to stay married for at least two years to get a second green card and, anecdotally and unfortunately, some of these couples separate after 3-4 years.

##### DV

There is also a diversity visa. The idea behind it is that USA wants to have a diverse set of cultures and communities and the DV is given to those foreign citizens who are under-represented in the USA.

Romania happens to be one such under-represented country, so there is this "Loteria Vizelor" (Visa Lottery) happening every year in Romania. It is purely a lottery, very much like the H-1B, but with lower chances and only for Romanians (and other included countries). There is a fixed number of visas and the type of that visa is DV. There is generally an easy path from DV to Green Card and it is an option for the people who are lucky to be in one of the under represented countries and lucky to get drawn in the lottery.

##### Dependent

Many of the visas above have a related "dependent" visa for spouse and family of the primary applicant. I will only ennumerate them here, but keep in mind that most of these visas only give you the right to be in the USA (exactly like a B-1/B-2), but not the right to work here without a further EAD card, which is different for each category.

- J-2 - Dependent of a J-1 visa (no work rights)
- F-2 - Dependent of a F-1 visa (EAD needed)
- H-4 - Dependant of a H-1B visa (EAD needed)
- L-2 - Dependent of a L-1 visa (work authorized)
- O-3 - Dependent of a O-1 visa (no work rights)

##### Others

There are also other visas that I will just rapid fire here, but won't go into details

- National visas
	- TN for Canada and Mexico
	- E-3 for Australia
	- H1-B1 Chile & Singapore
- E-2 - Investment visa
- R-1 - Religious visa

## My current visa situation

With the primer out of the way, let's talk about me and then get to the story.

I came to the United States a couple of times. When I joined for summer internships, I arrived on a J-1 visa, that was only valid for those 3-6 months and later expired. When I arrived to pitch my startup to YCombinator, I entered on a B-1/B-2 visa which is generally awarded for 10 years and is still valid.

Since I joined Apple, I am on the H-1B visa, which is my current visa, and when I got married, my wife got a H-4 visa, to replace her B-1/B-2 visa and finally be able to move and work here.

In order to get my H-1B visa, I applied once in 2016 and was rejected, then again in 2017 and was rejected, then again in 2018 and was approved, but then delayed for unknown reasons and then further delayed during the 2018-2019 government shutdown.

H-1B, like many others, is tricky because, while not tied to an employer, it is tied to being employed. In other words, if I were to get laid off, which happens very often in tech, I would have 60 days to find a new job. If I were unable to gain employment in those 60 days, both myself and my wife would be forced to pack our things leave the United States.

While the situation is better than the L-1 equivalent where a layoff is the end of the road, it does create this paradox where the holder is neither fully in the USA (because we can get kicked out on a whim) nor outside the USA (because it is very hard to maintain relevant ties).

One implication that not many people talk about is that, practically, the H-1B does not really allow a career gap in the form of a sabbatical. One cannot just resign from a job due to burnout and go travel through South America for a couple of months. This creates a couple of interesting dynamics, first being that you must be super careful about your physical and mental wellbeing, so that you do not burn out. The other one being that people generally time their job change with a long holiday in between positions to refresh and renew.

With that said, I fully embraced my current situation. I am incredibly grateful for the opportunity to live in the USA, experience the culture and work alongside the brightest minds on the planet. It is an opportunity that I do not take for granted and if it were to be taken away from me, while not ideal, I would happily leave, knowing that all of this was a great experience.

## The Executive Order

With all of that admin work out of the way, let's talk about what happened on September 19, 2025 and how that impacted me.

September 19 was a Friday and the executive order was sent out around lunch time. At OpenAI, we have an internal Slack channel where we post and discuss the latest news, generally AI related. Around 3 PM, someone posted an article about this order, mentioning that although it is not AI news, it may impact AI.

Personally, I did not have a lot of time at the time to really digest what the executive order wanted to say. Reading between the lines, it could have been one of the following interpretations:
- Everyone getting a new H-1B must now pay $100,000 if they wanted to accept it
- Everyone on H-1B must pay $100,000 / year to maintain their status
- Everyone on H-1B must pay $100,000 every time they renewed the visa or changed the employers
- Everyone on H-1B must pay $100,000 every time they wanted to enter the country

To me, the most obvious interpretation was the first one, which would not have affected me because I already have the visa. However, I could see the other interpretations as being equally true, which would have definitelly affected me. Paying $100,000/year being the best case scenario of the three of them, not because it was cheap but because from a psychological perspective it would be not much that one can do except leaving USA entirely, and also because it was very likely that the employer would absorb part of the cost. Having to pay $100,000 for every employer change would be a psychological issue because I would be stuck with the employer and I'd rather leave than not be free. Having to pay $100,000 per entry would have had a similar impact because I would not have gone to China for holiday or to Romania for Christmas anymore, for the forseeable future.

Around 5 PM (end of the day), our internal immigration communications told us to hang tight until they had more clarity on the issue themselves and they would share with us any news that they had as soon as they had it. This was not a great announcement, given that the order would go into effect at the midnight between Saturday and Sunday, so just a bit over 24 hours from then.

Around 6:15 PM, we received the following advisory from a third party immigration firm that OpenAI contracted:


> [!Lawyer Email] Email from Immigration firm: 6:15 PM
> Urgent Client Advisory: Immediate H-1B Travel Action**
> **Entry to U.S. Before September 20, 2025 at 11:59 p.m. EST Required**
>
> **If You Are in the U.S. Now**
>
> We strongly recommend that you do not travel internationally until further details and guidance are issued by the U.S. government. Leaving the U.S. at this time may put you at risk of being unable to reenter without the $100,000 supplemental fee being paid or a waiver being approved.
>
> **If You Are Currently Abroad**
>
> If you are outside the U.S. with an H-1B visa, we strongly recommend returning to the U.S. before 11:59 p.m. Eastern Time on Saturday, September 20, 2025, to avoid being subject to the new entry restriction.

Basically everyone on a H-1B has 26 hours to return to the USA or risk losing their job and livelihood.

Three hours later, 9:06 PM (so Saturday Eastern Time and less than 24 hours left until order is in effect), we received the following official communication from OpenAI.


> [!OpenAI's Email] OpenAI Email - 9:06 PM
>
> [...]
>
> The order will take effect at 12:01am ET on Sunday, September 21, 2025, and includes a mandatory $100,000 fee for most H-1B petitions involving individuals seeking to enter the U.S. The proclamation specifies that this fee is generally the employer’s responsibility, though final government guidance on implementation and case-by-case requirements is still pending.
>
> [...]
>
> **What this means for you**
>
> **If you’re currently outside of the U.S. on an H-1B visa**
>
> - We recommend returning to the U.S. as soon as possible and before 12:01am ET on September 21 to avoid being subject to the new restrictions.
> - [...] We will reimburse you for any additional, reasonable travel costs associated with your change in plans to return before the deadline.
>
> **If you’re currently inside of the U.S. on an H-1B visa**
> - We strongly recommend postponing any international travel for now, as the scope and enforcement of the proclamation are still evolving. Traveling abroad could lead to serious reentry complications, including being subject to the $100,000 fee.
>
> **Who is not affected at this time**
> - Those who have H-1B status and are already in the U.S. and plan to remain in the U.S.
> - All other visa types.
> - H-4 holders (we are still awaiting specific guidance).

Out of all the possible interpretations of the Executive Order, this one was, in my opinion, the least likely and the most damaging. What this basically says is that every entry to the USA may cost $100,000, which particularly affects Laura and me because we are traveling a fair bit.

We spent the Friday night discussing what we should do about our upcoming, prepaid trip to China and about our Christmas visit to Romania to see our families. My suggestion was to sit and wait, as these things usually evolve and change.

An interesting twist of this entire situation was that I was scheduled to go to Romania in that particular week-end. The final round of the Romanian Endurance Karting Masters competition was happening the following week-end, my team was 3rd in the championship and I really wanted to make sure that we stay on the podium. I wanted to travel to Romania a few days early to acclimatize, participate in a second karting competition and train with the team.

A few weeks earlier, Laura, somehow, had a bad feeling about this plan, and asked me not to leave because she feels that I won't be able to return and she'll be here alone. I eventually agreed that the entire plan was a bit suboptimal: I'd have to take two 16-hour flights to Romania, miss a couple of days of work in a really crucial crunch period, all for just two races. I decided that I would not go anymore and found a replacement, essentially losing my entry fee but saving the time and the money for flights, accommodation and practice.

If I were to have gone ahead with that plan, this would have become a really stressful situation and I would have most likely abandoned the plan, essentially wasting all of the money. However, since I did not do that, I could afford to sit and wait.

The final clarifying email from OpenAI arrived on Saturday, 6PM (3 hours before the executive order came in effect).

> [!OpenAI Email 2] OpenAI Email 2 - Saturday, 6:10 PM
> Hi everyone,
>
> The U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) has issued updated guidance on the President’s recent proclamation regarding H-1B visas.
>
> **Here’s what’s new**
>
> - **No action required for current visa holders.** The proclamation does not affect approved petitions or valid H-1B visas already issued.
> - **Only new H-1B filings face a fee.** Petitions filed after 12:01am ET on September 21 must include a $100,000 payment unless exempted by the Department of Homeland Security. Again, this does not apply to approved petitions or valid H-1B visas.
> - **Next steps are still unclear for lottery cases**. We are still awaiting further clarification regarding both currently pending and newly approved FY2026 H-1B Cap (lottery) cases.
>
> There is **no need to urgently change your travel plans anymore**, but we will continue to review upcoming international travel plans to be extra cautious as guidance may continue to evolve.

The only funny thing looking back is the "There is no need to urgently change your travel plans anymore" at the end of that email that was sent 3 hours before the executive order would have come into effect. By that time, everyone on an H-1B was either back, in the air, about to be back or totally oblivious of the crisis. In other words, the clarification, while useful, would have arrived way too late.

## Conclusion

There are a couple of things that we learned here:
1. I am lucky that I was not eventually affected by the change in rules for the H-1B visa
2. I should continue to not take my stay in the USA for granted. My immigration status is a privilege and it can be taken away from me and my family at any time
3. When a crisis like this hits, we should keep calm and not take rushed decisions
