I can drop a couple of my own thoughts, but I could be more wrong than I am right, and also I do not know you personally, so pick and choose whatever you think fits for your individual case.

1. ICT5 is amazing! At Apple, most people plateau at ICT4, which is considered a terminal level, so congrats for going one level beyond that. I think ICT5 is the sweet spot, and many people agree with me. You get all the freedom and power that you need to pursue whatever goal you want and none of the headaches that come from ICT6. The pay is about the same. It's a great level to be at. Think hard if you actually want to go ICT6, and what is your motivation to get there (also let me know, curios what drives you to keep going). Assuming ICT6 is still what you want, keep on reading.

  

2. If you are ICT5, make sure people know it. By that, I mean put it on your LinkedIn. ICT5 means Staff Software Engineer. Put that in your title (instead of Sr). If you want to add ICT5 to it, do that too. Levels are private at Apple, but people do check you out on LinkedIn when they work with you.

  

3. Make sure you have visibility to leadership and that the leadership cares about what you do. The closer in the ladder to director, the better. For example, does Frank Chu know you exist? If not, start with that. Does he know what you are working on and does he care about it? Ask him. If you have a natural way to ask him that (like office hours or some quarterly 1:1) do it next time. If you do not, then your goal is to create that opportunity for yourself to talk to Frank. Similarly to Benoit and to JG.

  

4. Do not make chasing level a goal when talking to your peers and managers. That is turn off-y. But mention it to your manager at least twice a year, so that he knows that you are interested (again, many people are not).

  

5. Work super hard and prioritize work over family and hobbies (do you even want to do that?) while still taking care of your health. Always measure impact (how much money did you save with X, how many users did you affect with Y, how much easier it is to do Z). If you feel like you don't have work to measure or if you feel like you do not have time to measure your work, then you are not working hard enough.

  

6. If your day-to-day still involves coding, aim to make 1 meaningful PR a day. That compounds very quickly and people take notice of your internal contribution graph (or at least they remember that they had to review 365 of your PRs this year). If you do not have enough work for 1 PR a day, see point 3. If you do not have time for 1 PR a day, see point 5.

  

7. If your day-to-day work is more tech lead-y, then you have to project confidence during meetings. If you are not confident about what you are saying, figure out why that is. If it is because of a personality trait, then is it even a good idea to go ICT6? If it is because of the technical nature of what you are doing, spend evenings and week-ends mastering whatever that is (see point 5). For example if you are not confident about how RAG should be integrated with the LLM, spend a few week-ends building your own RAG system at home.

  

8. Do not job hop. Job hopping will increase your pay, but you hit "reset" on your level every time you do it. Getting to ICT6 requires familiarity with the code and, more importantly, familiarity with the people. Unless you transfer with a bump, do not take offers that promise you that you will be level X in Y years because that will not happen. Apple is probably the fastest way if that is what you want. Check out LinkedIn. Everyone who became distinguished engineer at Google or Meta, did so after spending 10-15+ years with the company.

  

9. And probably the most important, do not care too much about anything. Chasing a role is just frustration and you do not control much of the process anyway. So just sit back, enjoy and do the best work of your life. It's also easier to do that when you have options, so always have options.

  

I hope this helps. Good luck! And let me know what drives your motivation to become ICT6.
