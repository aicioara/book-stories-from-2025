# Vasile Goldiș School

At the end of 2024, Vasile Goldiș, my primary school, reached out to me. They were about to celebrate their 100 year anniversary and they were putting together an anniversary book. They were hoping to find a couple of alumni volunteers who could write some articles for the book and I agreed to write my own (long) version of a Happy Birthday card. 

The final article can be found in the school library. It was written in Romanian and, after the book editor cut out the extra bits, it ended up much shorter than the version reproduced here. I think the original was much nicer, so I asked ChatGPT 5.2 to translate it to English. I am reproducing the final result here for your enjoyment.

In the context of this book, you are about to read the only article that was translated from Romanian into English, as opposed to being written directly by me in English. It is also the first time I ever used ChatGPT for translations. I hope that you enjoy!

---

I would like to wish Vasile Goldiș School a warm happy anniversary and to wish lots of success in life to all the students who have passed through, are passing through, and will pass through the doors of this school.

My name is Andrei Cioara and I am currently employed by Apple in California, United States of America. I am a Senior Software Engineer and Technology Lead for a series of Artificial Intelligence applications in Siri and other Apple products.

Before the 6 years spent here, I had the opportunity to also work for Microsoft, Palantir, and a few other financial institutions in London. My opening to the job market was offered by Imperial College London. My preparation to reach that university was given to me by Horea Cloșca și Crișan National College in Alba Iulia (HCC). And finally, or rather at the very beginning, the foundations on which all my education and this entire trajectory were built were laid during the 8 years I spent at Vasile Goldiș School.

I vaguely remember my first day of school. It was the fall of 2000 and I was probably holding flowers in my hand. Actually, my mom was probably holding them because I quickly made friends with a future classmate and was running around the schoolyard without a care in the world. On that day I met Mrs. Puican, the teacher who would guide us for the next 3 years.

Mrs. Doina Puican was famous for the experience she had accumulated throughout her teaching career and for the success rate of her former students. I do not know how my parents managed to make sure I was among the 25 students in her class, but I thank them for that effort. The first years of school build the foundation on which the rest of your career is constructed, and the years spent in Mrs. Puican's class defined me as a person. Among other things, I notice today that I still have the same handwriting I learned from Mrs. Puican, a writing style that I will probably carry with me my entire life.

The first three years of school were not free of worries. I lacked the dexterity to write nicely and especially to write quickly. All my classmates managed to fill an entire page with the letter "A" by hand by the time I could only write two lines. So that the whole class would not have to wait for me, the remaining lines became "extra homework for home". This taught me that it is normal to work more than those around you to compensate for a lack of talent, or in this case, a lack of dexterity.

By 3rd grade I managed to write just as fast as the rest of my classmates, so the extra homework disappeared. To fill the newly gained free time, I started solving problems from a workbook called "777 math problems" recommended by Mrs. Puican. It was a small book with problems, accompanied by a huge book with the solutions (which was supposed to be kept by the parents). My mom considered it important to trust a 9 year old child, so she left me the book with the solutions. I understood the responsibility and never looked at a solution before managing to solve a problem on my own, but I always checked immediately after finishing whether my solution was correct, and when it was not, I quickly learned why.

The experience with "777 problems" taught me many things. Most importantly, I understood discipline: just because you have access to something does not mean you should use it. Around that time I also experienced for the first time the phenomenon of being 100% absorbed in what you are doing. Americans call it "deep work". The problems in the workbook were much harder than those in class, and sometimes I spent hours on a single solution. I could not read the solution, but I knew one existed, and that curiosity pushed me to understand more and more mathematics.

Sometimes people ask me what was useful in life from everything I learned in school. My answer is in the paragraphs above: the humility to realize that a lack of talent can be compensated through work, the discipline to not take advantage of the power you are given, and the perseverance to stay with a single problem for as long as it takes to solve it.

Mrs. Ofelia Corpodean took over our class for the last year of primary school, 4th grade, when Mrs. Puican retired. We transitioned from a highly experienced teacher nearing retirement, who had built us a solid foundation, to a young teacher at the beginning of her career. The transition was very smooth and, in my memories, things continued as if nothing had happened.

Mrs. Corpodean introduced us to the world of competitions. There were competitions in mathematics, Romanian language, civic education, all kinds. Having all the mathematical experience from the "777 problems" book from previous years, I had a significant advantage in math competitions and managed to win a few first places. That is how I discovered my passion for competition, and I thank Mrs. Corpodean for steering away from the "everyone gets first place" mentality that was popular at the time.

5th grade introduced a new element, unique to Vasile Goldiș School: intensive English classes. In a special partnership between the school and the Peace Corps, volunteers from the United States of America came to Romania for periods of one year and taught us English by speaking to us in their native language. Obviously, these teachers did not speak Romanian at all, and the fact that an 11 year old child could not only understand them, but actually have fluent conversations and become attached to them was fascinating. Miss Elaine was a unique experience for me, being the first foreign person I ever met and to whom, just like my classmates, I became attached.

To get into this class with limited spots, at the end of 4th grade we had to pass a selection exam in English. This exam, both then and in the years that followed, was controversial, with many parents believing that an 11 year old should not be given the responsibility of an exam. Regardless of opinions, what this exam clearly did was concentrate the most talented children from the entire city into just two classes, and I was lucky enough to be among them.

The fact that 22 out of the 25 of my classmates from grades 1-4 passed the exam says a lot about the success of Mrs. Puican and Mrs. Corpodean, whom I thank for their guidance during our formative years. At the same time, this result says just as much about the extraordinary English teacher we had.

Mrs. Elizabeta Cisteian was our English teacher from 1st grade through 8th grade. The fact that, despite my lack of interest and lack of talent for foreign languages, Mrs. Cisteian managed to lay the foundations of this language for me and managed (together with extra tutoring from my mom) to help me pass the English exam in 4th grade is nothing short of miraculous.

Many people ask me what the most important subject I learned in school was. My answer, without hesitation, is English. This subject helped me get to London and later to the United States. This subject helped me understand how computers work and what error messages mean (which in the 2000s were not translated into Romanian). English helped me travel all over the world and manage on my own. Most importantly, English was, is, and will be the most important subject because all the information on the planet is on the internet, and the internet is predominantly in English. Even when applications like ChatGPT and Artificial Intelligence will democratize this information for all the languages of the planet (probably in the next 5 years), the world's elite will continue to speak English, the latest discoveries will continue to be published in English, and those who do not know the language will automatically be excluded from the truly important conversations.

So I thank Mrs. Cisteian once again for laying the foundations of the language, I thank the other English teachers I had, both at Vasile Goldiș School and at HCC, and I thank Mrs. Puican once again for convincing Mrs. Cisteian to teach us this subject starting in 1st grade, when we children did not even know how to read or write.

If the foundations were laid in grades 1-4, consolidation happened in grades 5-8. Some subjects I liked. Others less so. Still, the teachers I was lucky enough to work with were extraordinary. I will mention only the two subjects that stayed closest to my heart: mathematics and geography.

Mrs. Gabriela Ursu was our mathematics teacher and homeroom teacher, and she is the person who made me love mathematics. People generally end up following a profession because of a passion for a certain subject. Additionally, what I have observed is that people usually receive that passion for a subject from the teacher who taught it.

For Software Engineering (my profession), the most important component is mathematics. I feel lucky that Mrs. Ursu was my teacher during the most important years of my mathematical development and that she explained concepts so well that even today I remember, in detail, all the geometry lessons. Without her, I would probably have concluded that my interest in mathematics was just the "777 problems" workbook and that I should choose a different path in life. It is hard for me to imagine life doing anything other than Software Engineering, so I thank Mrs. Ursu for igniting a love for mathematics both in me and in other classmates.

Mrs. Floare Lăncrănjan was our geography teacher in grades 5-8 and, besides geography, she showed me two very important things: authority and performance. Mrs. Lăncrănjan was the only teacher for whom absolute silence would fall when she entered the classroom. Nobody moved in their desk. Imagine 30 children absolutely still and attentive to the lesson, and now add the fact that those children are 11 years old. I wish I could have learned that kind of authority myself, it would have helped me a lot in life, but I never managed to.

What I did learn from Mrs. Lăncrănjan, however, is performance. In 8th grade, after three failed attempts to reach the national mathematics olympiad, I decided to try geography. The change was a risk and an exclusive decision, because the county-level math and geography olympiads were held at the same time that year. What followed was a new experience for me. I received exclusive preparation attention from one of the most talented teachers at Vasile Goldiș School. The competition was no longer with other classmates, it was with myself. To know more geography today than I knew yesterday.

In the weeks before the olympiad, I learned more about sheep in the Apuseni Mountains than I thought was possible, and after the county olympiad (where I wrote from the moment I received the subjects until we were forced to put our pens down) and a tie-breaking exam, I was declared the winner. That is how I reached my first national olympiad, where I also managed to win my first national prize (an honorable mention). If until then I believed that only talent was enough in life and that work was needed only to compensate for a lack of talent in some areas, once I reached the national level, I understood that true performance requires a combination of talent and work. I want to thank Mrs. Lăncrănjan for the opportunity to compare myself with the elite of the country in geography. At the same time, I thank her for teaching me how performance is achieved, for the special attention she gave me during the preparation period for the olympiad, and of course, for the fact that almost 20 years later I still remember that our mountains are predominantly made of crystalline schists.

Finally, I also want to thank the physical education teacher, Mr. Pușcaș, who taught me that even though there are subjects we are not good at, it is more important to try than to throw in the towel and get a medical excuse. I thank him for not letting me give up on sports, for not "ruining my grade average", and for allowing me to focus on the subjects I considered important at the time. If he reads these lines, I want him to know that today I go to the gym 5 times a week, that I have run several long-distance races, and that I have pursued performance in karting races. Even though I was not good at this subject during my school years, physical education and sports are, for me, the third most important subject in school, after English and everyone's favorite subject (for me, mathematics).

For me, it was a nostalgic experience to go through this list of memories in order to put these words on paper. I remember fondly the formative years of my life and, although I managed to mention only a few teachers by name, I want to thank all the teachers at Vasile Goldiș School, because each of them contributed to my development into the person I am today. Happy anniversary, Vasile Goldiș School!
